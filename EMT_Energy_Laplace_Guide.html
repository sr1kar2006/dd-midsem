3. Part A: Electrostatic energy and energy density

3.1 The core idea: energy = work done to assemble the charges

Charges push or pull each other, so bringing charges together takes work. Electrostatic fields are conservative: the work done doesn't depend on the path, and it isn't lost. It is stored as potential energy in the arrangement. That stored energy is \(W_e\) (the subscript e stands for "electric").

The bookkeeping uses the definition of potential: V at a point = work per unit charge to bring a charge from infinity to that point. So bringing charge \(Q\) to a place where the potential is \(V\) costs work \(QV\).

3.2 Building it up one charge at a time

1
First charge \(Q_1\). Space is empty, so there is no field to fight. Work = 0.
2
Second charge \(Q_2\), brought to distance \(R_{12}\) from \(Q_1\). It moves against the field of \(Q_1\): \[W_2=Q_2V_2=Q_2\frac{Q_1}{4\pi\epsilon_0R_{12}}\qquad\text{(Cheng 3-159)}\] The expression is symmetric, so it is also \(Q_1V_1\). Averaging the two gives \(W_2=\tfrac12(Q_1V_1+Q_2V_2)\).
3
Third charge \(Q_3\) must fight both: \[\Delta W=Q_3\left(\frac{Q_1}{4\pi\epsilon_0R_{13}}+\frac{Q_2}{4\pi\epsilon_0R_{23}}\right)\] \[W_3=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left(\frac{Q_1Q_2}{R_{12}}+\frac{Q_1Q_3}{R_{13}}+\frac{Q_2Q_3}{R_{23}}\right)=\tfrac12(Q_1V_1+Q_2V_2+Q_3V_3)\]
Formula: N discrete point charges (Cheng 3-165) \[ W_e=\frac12\sum_{k=1}^{N}Q_kV_k \quad\text{(J)},\qquad V_k=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{j\ne k}\frac{Q_j}{R_{jk}} \] Equivalent "sum over pairs" form (easier for numbers; every pair counted once): \[ W_e=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{\text{all pairs } i\lt j}\frac{Q_iQ_j}{R_{ij}} \]
Interpretation: why the ½?In \(\sum Q_kV_k\) every pair is counted twice: once as "\(Q_1\) sitting in \(Q_2\)'s potential" and once as "\(Q_2\) sitting in \(Q_1\)'s potential". The ½ removes the double counting.
Two remarks from the textbook (often asked as theory)
  1. \(W_e\) can be negative. With opposite charges the field does the work: the charges attract and you hold them back as they come in. A negative total energy means a bound system.
  2. The discrete formula gives only the interaction (mutual) energy. It leaves out the self-energy, the work needed to build each point charge itself. For a true point charge the self-energy is infinite, since it behaves like \(Q^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0 a)\) with \(a\to0\).

Electron-volt: \(1\ \text{eV}=1.60\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}\). It is the energy gained by an electron moving through a potential difference of 1 V. To convert J to eV, divide by \(1.6\times10^{-19}\).

3.3 Continuous charge distributions

Replace \(Q_k\to\rho\,dv\) and the sum by an integral:

Formula (Cheng 3-170) \[W_e=\frac12\int_{V'}\rho V\,dv\quad\text{(J)}\] Here \(V\) is the potential at the location of \(\rho\,dv\). This version includes self-energy, because every infinitesimal piece interacts with every other piece. Analogous forms: \(\tfrac12\oint\rho_sV\,ds\) for surface charge and \(\tfrac12\int\rho_\ell V\,d\ell\) for line charge.

Special case: a charged conductor. The whole conductor is at one potential \(V\), so \(W_e=\tfrac12 V\oint\rho_s\,ds=\tfrac12QV\).

3.4 Energy in terms of the FIELD (the most important form)

Often we know \(\mathbf E\) but not \(\rho\). Derivation (worth knowing, as it is sometimes asked):

1
Put \(\rho=\nabla\cdot\mathbf D\) into \(W_e=\tfrac12\int\rho V\,dv\): \(\;W_e=\tfrac12\int(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D)V\,dv\).
2
Vector identity: \(\nabla\cdot(V\mathbf D)=V\nabla\cdot\mathbf D+\mathbf D\cdot\nabla V\), so \((\nabla\cdot\mathbf D)V=\nabla\cdot(V\mathbf D)-\mathbf D\cdot\nabla V\).
3
Divergence theorem on the first term: \(W_e=\tfrac12\oint_{S'}V\mathbf D\cdot\mathbf a_n\,ds+\tfrac12\int\mathbf D\cdot\mathbf E\,dv\) (using \(-\nabla V=\mathbf E\)).
4
Let the surface be a huge sphere of radius \(R\to\infty\). Then \(V\sim1/R\), \(D\sim1/R^2\) and area \(\sim R^2\), so the surface term \(\sim 1/R\to0\). What remains is:
Formula (Cheng 3-176 a, b, c) \[ W_e=\frac12\int_{V'}\mathbf D\cdot\mathbf E\,dv=\frac12\int_{V'}\epsilon E^2\,dv=\frac12\int_{V'}\frac{D^2}{\epsilon}\,dv\qquad\text{(J)} \]

3.5 Energy density \(w_e\)

Define \(w_e\) so that \(W_e=\int_{V'}w_e\,dv\). From the above:

Formula: energy density (Cheng 3-178 a, b, c) \[ \boxed{\,w_e=\tfrac12\mathbf D\cdot\mathbf E=\tfrac12\epsilon E^2=\frac{D^2}{2\epsilon}\quad(\text{J/m}^3)\,} \]
Interpretations you should be able to write in words
  • Energy is pictured as stored in the field itself, spread through space, with the most energy where \(E\) is strongest.
  • \(w_e\propto E^2\): doubling the field quadruples the energy density.
  • For a given E, a higher \(\epsilon\) stores more energy (\(\tfrac12\epsilon E^2\)). For a given D, a higher \(\epsilon\) stores less (\(D^2/2\epsilon\)). Which form to use depends on what is held fixed.
  • Cheng's caveat: this definition is a mathematical convenience. No experiment can locate energy at a particular point. Only the total integral \(W_e\) is physically guaranteed.
  • Units check: \(\epsilon E^2\) has units (F/m)(V/m)² = F·V²/m³ = J/m³, since F·V² = C·V = J.
  • Energy density is also a pressure: \(\tfrac12\epsilon E^2\) is the outward force per unit area (N/m²) on a conductor surface. See Part A2.

3.6 Capacitor energy

Parallel plates (Cheng Ex. 3-24): \(E=V/d\) is uniform, and the volume is \(Sd\):

\[ W_e=\tfrac12\epsilon\left(\frac Vd\right)^2(Sd)=\tfrac12\left(\frac{\epsilon S}{d}\right)V^2 \]
Formula: true for ANY two-conductor capacitor (Cheng 3-180) \[ W_e=\tfrac12CV^2=\tfrac12QV=\frac{Q^2}{2C}\quad\text{(J)} \]
Exam trick: capacitance from energy (Cheng Ex. 3-25)
  1. Assume charge \(Q\) and find \(\mathbf E\) by Gauss's law.
  2. Compute \(W_e=\tfrac12\int\epsilon E^2\,dv\).
  3. Set it equal to \(Q^2/2C\) and solve: \(\;C=\dfrac{Q^2}{2W_e}\) (or \(C=2W_e/V^2\)).
For coax this gives \(W_e=\dfrac{Q^2}{4\pi\epsilon L}\ln\dfrac ba\Rightarrow C=\dfrac{2\pi\epsilon L}{\ln(b/a)}\).

4. Part A2: Electrostatic forces from energy (virtual displacement)

Finding forces with Coulomb's law is tedious for conductors. Instead: imagine moving a body by a tiny distance \(d\boldsymbol\ell\) (a "virtual displacement") and see how the energy changes.

Case 1: Fixed charges (isolated system, no battery)

The mechanical work done by the field must come out of the stored energy: \(\mathbf F_Q\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell=-dW_e=-(\nabla W_e)\cdot d\boldsymbol\ell\).

Formula (Cheng 3-185)\[\mathbf F_Q=-\nabla W_e\quad\text{(N)}\qquad\text{e.g. } (F_Q)_x=-\frac{\partial W_e}{\partial x}\]

Case 2: Fixed potentials (battery connected)

Now the battery supplies energy \(dW_s=\sum V_k\,dQ_k\). Half of it goes into stored energy and half into mechanical work: \(dW_e=\tfrac12dW_s\), and so \(dW=dW_e\).

Formula\[\mathbf F_V=+\nabla W_e\quad\text{(N)}\]
InterpretationBoth formulas give the same physical force. The sign differs only because \(W_e\) is written in terms of different held-constant quantities (\(Q\) in one case, \(V\) in the other). Bodies move so as to decrease \(W_e\) when charge is fixed and to increase \(W_e\) when potential is fixed. Either way, they move so as to increase capacitance.

Force between capacitor plates

\[ |F|=\frac{Q^2}{2\epsilon S}=\frac{\epsilon SV^2}{2d^2}=\tfrac12\epsilon E^2\,S=\frac{\rho_s^2}{2\epsilon}S\qquad(\text{attractive}) \]
Electrostatic pressure on a conductor surface\[p=\frac{F}{S}=\frac{\rho_s^2}{2\epsilon}=\tfrac12\epsilon E^2=w_e\quad(\text{N/m}^2)\] The force is always directed outward from the conductor, into the field region. For a capacitor that means each plate is pulled toward the other.

Torque version for rotation: \((T_Q)_z=-\dfrac{\partial W_e}{\partial\phi}\) (fixed charge), \((T_V)_z=+\dfrac{\partial W_e}{\partial\phi}\) (fixed V).

5. Part B: Poisson's and Laplace's equations

5.1 Why do we need them?

In Chapter 3 you found V and E from a known charge distribution. In real devices (capacitors, cables, electrodes), you usually know the potentials of the conductors ("this plate is at 100 V, that one is grounded"), but you don't know how charge distributes itself on them. That's a boundary-value problem: solve a differential equation in the region between conductors, subject to the known boundary potentials.

5.2 Derivation (3 lines; learn it)

1
Gauss: \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho\), with \(\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E\), so \(\nabla\cdot(\epsilon\mathbf E)=\rho\).
2
Irrotational field: \(\nabla\times\mathbf E=0\), so \(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\). Hence \(\nabla\cdot(\epsilon\nabla V)=-\rho\).
3
Simple medium (linear, isotropic, homogeneous: \(\epsilon\) constant) lets \(\epsilon\) come out:
Poisson's equation (Cheng 4-6)\[\nabla^2V=-\frac{\rho}{\epsilon}\qquad(\text{V/m}^2)\]
Laplace's equation (Cheng 4-10): charge-free region, \(\rho=0\)\[\nabla^2V=0\]

\(\nabla^2\) ("del squared", the Laplacian) means "divergence of the gradient": \(\nabla^2V=\nabla\cdot\nabla V\). It's a second-order PDE.

Physical meaning of the Laplacian\(\nabla^2V\) at a point measures how much \(V\) there differs from the average of \(V\) in its immediate neighbourhood. \(\nabla^2V=0\) means "V at each point equals the average of its surroundings": no bumps, no dips. Charge is what creates bumps or dips. Positive \(\rho\) gives \(\nabla^2 V<0\), a local hill in V.

5.3 The Laplacian in all three coordinate systems

Memorise (Cheng 4-7, 4-8, 4-9) \[\text{Cartesian: }\ \nabla^2V=\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial x^2}+\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial y^2}+\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2}\] \[\text{Cylindrical: }\ \nabla^2V=\frac1r\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\right)+\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial\phi^2}+\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2}\] \[\text{Spherical: }\ \nabla^2V=\frac{1}{R^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial R}\!\left(R^2\frac{\partial V}{\partial R}\right)+\frac{1}{R^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}\right)+\frac{1}{R^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial\phi^2}\]
Memory aidRadial parts: cylindrical has \(r\) inside and outside (\(\frac1r\,\partial_r\, r\,\partial_r\)). Spherical has \(R^2\) inside and outside (\(\frac1{R^2}\,\partial_R\, R^2\,\partial_R\)). The \(\theta\) term mirrors that pattern with \(\sin\theta\).

5.5 Laplace vs Poisson: which one?

Decision rule
  • Region between conductors with no charge (vacuum or ideal dielectric): Laplace.
  • Region containing volume charge (electron cloud, space charge in a diode, a p-n junction): Poisson.
  • Surface charges on conductors are not volume charge. They live on the boundary and are handled through boundary conditions. So a capacitor is a Laplace problem even though its plates are charged.

6. Part B2: Uniqueness theorem and properties of harmonic functions

Uniqueness theorem (Cheng 4-3) A solution of Poisson's equation (Laplace's equation is the special case \(\rho=0\)) that satisfies the given boundary conditions is the only solution.

Proof idea (sometimes asked): suppose \(V_1\) and \(V_2\) both work. Let \(V_d=V_1-V_2\). Then \(\nabla^2V_d=0\) inside and \(V_d=0\) on the conductors. Using the identity \(\nabla\cdot(V_d\nabla V_d)=V_d\nabla^2V_d+|\nabla V_d|^2\) and the divergence theorem, \(\oint V_d\nabla V_d\cdot d\mathbf s=\int|\nabla V_d|^2dv\). The left side is zero because \(V_d=0\) on the boundary, and also at infinity. So \(\int|\nabla V_d|^2dv=0\), which forces \(\nabla V_d=0\) everywhere. \(V_d\) is then a constant, and that constant is 0 because \(V_d=0\) on the boundary. So \(V_1=V_2\).

Why it matters (interpretation)
  • You may guess a solution by any method: inspection, symmetry, images, separation of variables. If it satisfies the equation and the boundary conditions, it is THE answer.
  • This is the justification for the method of images.

Properties of solutions of Laplace's equation

  1. Mean-value property: \(V\) at a point = average of \(V\) over any sphere (or circle, in 2-D) centred there that encloses no charge.
  2. No local maxima or minima inside a charge-free region. The extremes of V occur on the boundaries (conductors).
  3. Consequence, Earnshaw's theorem: a charge can't be held in stable equilibrium by electrostatic forces alone.
  4. Superposition: Laplace's equation is linear, so if \(V_1\) and \(V_2\) are solutions, so is \(aV_1+bV_2\). (This is used in separation of variables.)
  5. Laplace's equation alone gives nothing until boundary conditions are imposed. The boundary conditions fix the integration constants.

7. Part B3: Solving Laplace's equation, the 1-D cases

The universal 6-step recipe (use this for every problem)
1
Pick the coordinate system that matches the conductor shapes: planes → Cartesian, coaxial cylinders or wedges → cylindrical, concentric spheres or cones → spherical.
2
Decide which ONE coordinate V depends on, by symmetry and by neglecting fringing. Drop all derivatives with respect to the others.
3
Write the reduced ODE and integrate twice. This gives two constants \(C_1, C_2\).
4
Apply the two boundary conditions (the two conductor potentials) to find \(C_1\) and \(C_2\).
5
\(\mathbf E=-\nabla V\) (mind the scale factors), then \(\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E\).
6
\(\rho_s=\mathbf a_n\cdot\mathbf D\) on each conductor (\(\mathbf a_n\) points away from the conductor). Then \(Q=\int\rho_s\,ds\), and \(C=Q/V_0\).

7.3 Poisson examples (when charge is present)

(a) Uniform charge \(\rho_0\) between grounded plates at \(y=0\) and \(y=d\): \(\dfrac{d^2V}{dy^2}=-\dfrac{\rho_0}{\epsilon}\Rightarrow V=-\dfrac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon}y^2+C_1y+C_2\). With \(V(0)=V(d)=0\): \(\;V=\dfrac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon}(yd-y^2)\), a parabola with maximum \(\rho_0d^2/8\epsilon\) at the centre. (Q15 below.)

(b) Vacuum diode, space-charge limited (Cheng Ex. 4-2): cathode at \(y=0\) (V=0), anode at \(y=d\) (\(V_0\)), with electrons leaving the cathode at zero speed.

Results \[V(y)=V_0\left(\frac yd\right)^{4/3},\qquad J=\frac{4\epsilon_0}{9d^2}\sqrt{\frac{2e}{m}}\;V_0^{3/2}\quad\text{(Child–Langmuir law)}\] Interpretation: the diode is non-linear (\(J\propto V_0^{3/2}\)), unlike Ohm's law. (Full derivation in Q16.)

(c) Spherical electron cloud of uniform density \(-\rho_0\), radius b (Cheng Ex. 4-3): inside, Poisson gives \(\mathbf E=-\mathbf a_R\dfrac{\rho_0R}{3\epsilon_0}\). Outside, Laplace gives \(\mathbf E=-\mathbf a_R\dfrac{\rho_0b^3}{3\epsilon_0R^2}\). The constants come from "E finite at R = 0" and "E continuous at R = b".

8. Part B4: Separation of variables (when V depends on two coordinates)

When V depends on two coordinates (say x and y), guess \(V(x,y)=X(x)\,Y(y)\). Substituting into \(\nabla^2V=0\) and dividing by \(XY\):

\[\frac{1}{X}\frac{d^2X}{dx^2}+\frac{1}{Y}\frac{d^2Y}{dy^2}=0\]

The first term depends only on x and the second only on y, and they sum to zero for all x and y. So each must be a constant: \(\dfrac{X''}{X}=-k^2,\ \dfrac{Y''}{Y}=+k^2\) (or the opposite sign). \(k\) is the separation constant.

\(k^2\)\(X(x)\)Shape and when you need it
\(X''=0\) (\(k=0\))\(A_0x+B_0\)Linear
\(X''=-k^2X\)\(A\sin kx+B\cos kx\)Oscillating. Use in the direction where V = 0 on two parallel boundaries (so you can fit zeros).
\(X''=+k^2X\)\(Ae^{kx}+Be^{-kx}\) or \(A\sinh kx+B\cosh kx\)Growing or decaying. Use in the direction going to infinity (\(e^{-kx}\)) or a finite box (\(\sinh\)).

Sum of the separation constants: \(k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2=0\) (in 3-D), so at least one must be imaginary. In other words, at least one direction oscillates and at least one grows or decays.

Classic problem: the grounded semi-infinite slot

Two grounded half-planes at \(y=0\) and \(y=b\) extend from \(x=0\) to \(x=\infty\). The end at \(x=0\) is an insulated strip held at \(V_0\). Find V in the slot. (Fully worked in Q21.)

\[V(x,y)=\frac{4V_0}{\pi}\sum_{n=1,3,5,\dots}\frac{1}{n}\,e^{-n\pi x/b}\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b}\]

Advanced results in cylindrical and spherical coordinates (recognise them)

  • Cylindrical, \(V(r,\phi)\): separated solutions are \(r^{\pm n}(\sin n\phi,\cos n\phi)\). A conducting cylinder of radius a placed in a uniform field \(\mathbf E_0=\mathbf a_xE_0\) gives \(V=-E_0\left(r-\dfrac{a^2}{r}\right)\cos\phi\), with \(\rho_s=2\epsilon_0E_0\cos\phi\).
  • Spherical, \(V(R,\theta)\): solutions are \(R^n P_n(\cos\theta)\) and \(R^{-(n+1)}P_n(\cos\theta)\), where \(P_n\) are the Legendre polynomials \(P_0=1,\ P_1=\cos\theta,\ P_2=\tfrac12(3\cos^2\theta-1)\). A conducting sphere of radius b in a uniform field \(\mathbf a_zE_0\) gives \(V=-E_0\left(1-\dfrac{b^3}{R^3}\right)R\cos\theta\), with \(\rho_s=3\epsilon_0E_0\cos\theta\).

Show step-by-step solution

First, find E by Gauss's law (we'll need it):

  • Inside (\(R<b\)): enclosed charge \(=\rho\cdot\tfrac43\pi R^3\). Then \(E\cdot4\pi R^2=\dfrac{\rho\frac43\pi R^3}{\epsilon_0}\Rightarrow E_1=\dfrac{\rho R}{3\epsilon_0}\).

(a) Using \(\tfrac12\int\rho V\,dv\). We need V inside.

1
\(V(R)=-\int_\infty^bE_2\,dR-\int_b^RE_1\,dR=\dfrac{\rho b^2}{3\epsilon_0}+\dfrac{\rho}{6\epsilon_0}(b^2-R^2)=\dfrac{\rho}{3\epsilon_0}\left(\dfrac32b^2-\dfrac{R^2}{2}\right)\).
2
\(W_e=\dfrac{\rho}{2}\int_0^b\dfrac{\rho}{3\epsilon_0}\left(\dfrac32b^2-\dfrac{R^2}2\right)4\pi R^2dR\).
3
\(\int_0^b\left(\tfrac32b^2R^2-\tfrac12R^4\right)dR=\tfrac12b^5-\tfrac1{10}b^5=\tfrac25b^5\).
4
\(W_e=\dfrac{\rho^2}{6\epsilon_0}\cdot4\pi\cdot\dfrac25b^5=\dfrac{4\pi\rho^2b^5}{15\epsilon_0}\).
5
With \(\rho=\dfrac{3Q}{4\pi b^3}\): \(W_e=\dfrac{4\pi b^5}{15\epsilon_0}\cdot\dfrac{9Q^2}{16\pi^2b^6}=\dfrac{3Q^2}{20\pi\epsilon_0b}\).

(b) Using \(\tfrac12\int\epsilon_0E^2dv\), split into the two regions:

1
Inside: \(W_{in}=\tfrac12\epsilon_0\int_0^b\left(\dfrac{QR}{4\pi\epsilon_0b^3}\right)^24\pi R^2dR=\dfrac{Q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0b^6}\int_0^bR^4dR=\dfrac{Q^2}{40\pi\epsilon_0b}\).

3
Total: \(\dfrac{Q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0b}\left(1+\dfrac15\right)=\dfrac{6Q^2}{40\pi\epsilon_0b}=\dfrac{3Q^2}{20\pi\epsilon_0b}\). This matches (a).

(c) Fraction inside \(=\dfrac{1/40}{6/40}=\dfrac16\).

Answer: \(W_e=\dfrac{3Q^2}{20\pi\epsilon_0b}=\dfrac{4\pi\rho^2b^5}{15\epsilon_0}\). One sixth of it is stored inside the sphere and five sixths outside.

Q15. likely (Poisson)
The region \(0<y<d\) between two grounded plates holds a uniform charge density \(\rho_0\). Find V(y), E(y), the maximum potential, and its location.
Show step-by-step solution
1
Charge is present, so use Poisson: \(\dfrac{d^2V}{dy^2}=-\dfrac{\rho_0}{\epsilon}\).
2
Integrate once: \(\dfrac{dV}{dy}=-\dfrac{\rho_0}{\epsilon}y+C_1\). Integrate again: \(V=-\dfrac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon}y^2+C_1y+C_2\).
3
\(V(0)=0\Rightarrow C_2=0\). \(V(d)=0\Rightarrow-\dfrac{\rho_0d^2}{2\epsilon}+C_1d=0\Rightarrow C_1=\dfrac{\rho_0d}{2\epsilon}\).
4
\(V(y)=\dfrac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon}(yd-y^2)=\dfrac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon}y(d-y)\).
5
\(\mathbf E=-\dfrac{dV}{dy}\mathbf a_y=-\dfrac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon}(d-2y)\,\mathbf a_y=\dfrac{\rho_0}{\epsilon}\left(y-\dfrac d2\right)\mathbf a_y\). E is zero at the centre and points outward toward both plates (for \(\rho_0>0\)).
6
Maximum: \(dV/dy=0\Rightarrow y=d/2\). \(V_{max}=\dfrac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon}\cdot\dfrac d2\cdot\dfrac d2=\dfrac{\rho_0d^2}{8\epsilon}\).

Answer: \(V=\dfrac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon}y(d-y)\), with \(V_{max}=\dfrac{\rho_0d^2}{8\epsilon}\) at the midplane.

Note that this V has an interior maximum. That is allowed only because charge is present: with Poisson, unlike Laplace, interior maxima can occur.

Q16. likely (Cheng Ex 4-2)
Vacuum diode: cathode at \(y=0\) (V = 0), anode at \(y=d\) (\(V=V_0\)). Electrons leave the cathode with zero velocity and the field at the cathode is zero (space-charge limited). Derive V(y) and the current density J.
Show step-by-step solution
1
The electrons are a volume charge, so use Poisson: \(\dfrac{d^2V}{dy^2}=-\dfrac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}\), where \(\rho\) is negative.
2
Electron speed from energy conservation: \(\tfrac12mu^2=eV\Rightarrow u=\sqrt{2eV/m}\).
3
Current is the same everywhere in the steady state: \(J=|\rho|u\Rightarrow\rho=-\dfrac{J}{u}=-\dfrac{J}{\sqrt{2e/m}}V^{-1/2}\).
4
Poisson becomes \(\dfrac{d^2V}{dy^2}=\dfrac{J}{\epsilon_0\sqrt{2e/m}}V^{-1/2}\equiv K\,V^{-1/2}\).
5
Trick: multiply both sides by \(2\dfrac{dV}{dy}\). The left side becomes \(\dfrac{d}{dy}\left(\dfrac{dV}{dy}\right)^2\) and the right side becomes \(4K\dfrac{d}{dy}\left(V^{1/2}\right)\).
6
Integrate. At \(y=0\), both V and dV/dy are 0, so the constant is 0: \(\left(\dfrac{dV}{dy}\right)^2=4KV^{1/2}\Rightarrow\dfrac{dV}{dy}=2\sqrt K\,V^{1/4}\).
7
Separate: \(V^{-1/4}dV=2\sqrt K\,dy\Rightarrow\tfrac43V^{3/4}=2\sqrt K\,y\) (the constant is 0 since V(0) = 0).
8
At \(y=d\): \(\tfrac43V_0^{3/4}=2\sqrt Kd\). Divide the two: \(\left(\dfrac{V}{V_0}\right)^{3/4}=\dfrac yd\Rightarrow V=V_0\left(\dfrac yd\right)^{4/3}\).
9
From step 8, \(\sqrt K=\dfrac{2V_0^{3/4}}{3d}\Rightarrow K=\dfrac{4V_0^{3/2}}{9d^2}\). Since \(K=\dfrac{J}{\epsilon_0\sqrt{2e/m}}\), \(\;J=\dfrac{4\epsilon_0}{9d^2}\sqrt{\dfrac{2e}{m}}\,V_0^{3/2}\).

Answer: \(V=V_0(y/d)^{4/3}\) and \(J=\dfrac{4\epsilon_0}{9d^2}\sqrt{\dfrac{2e}{m}}V_0^{3/2}\) (the Child–Langmuir three-halves power law).

Q21. advanced, possible
Two grounded, semi-infinite parallel conducting planes lie at \(y=0\) and \(y=b\) for \(x>0\). The end \(x=0\) is closed by a conducting strip, insulated from the planes, held at \(V_0\). Find V(x, y) inside the slot.
Show step-by-step solution
1
Boundary conditions (no z dependence, so 2-D): (a) V(x, 0) = 0, (b) V(x, b) = 0, (c) V → 0 as x → ∞, (d) V(0, y) = V₀.
2
Separate: V = X(x)Y(y), so \(X''/X=-Y''/Y=k^2\).
3
Choose the forms. In y, V must be zero at y = 0 and y = b, which needs a sine: \(Y=\sin ky\). In x, V must decay, so \(X=e^{-kx}\) (we discard \(e^{+kx}\) because it blows up).
4
Apply (b): \(\sin kb=0\Rightarrow k=\dfrac{n\pi}{b}\), n = 1, 2, 3, …
5
Superpose (Laplace is linear): \(V=\sum_n C_n e^{-n\pi x/b}\sin\dfrac{n\pi y}{b}\).
6
Apply (d) at x = 0: \(V_0=\sum C_n\sin\dfrac{n\pi y}{b}\) for 0 < y < b. This is a Fourier sine series. Multiply by \(\sin\frac{m\pi y}{b}\) and integrate from 0 to b. Orthogonality leaves \(C_m\dfrac b2=V_0\int_0^b\sin\dfrac{m\pi y}{b}dy=\dfrac{V_0b}{m\pi}(1-\cos m\pi)\).
7
\(C_n=\dfrac{2V_0}{n\pi}(1-\cos n\pi)\). That gives \(\dfrac{4V_0}{n\pi}\) for odd n and 0 for even n.

Answer: \(V(x,y)=\dfrac{4V_0}{\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1,3,5,\dots}\dfrac1n\,e^{-n\pi x/b}\sin\dfrac{n\pi y}{b}\).

Interpretation: each Fourier mode decays exponentially into the slot, and higher modes decay faster. Far inside, only the n = 1 term matters: \(V\approx\frac{4V_0}{\pi}e^{-\pi x/b}\sin\frac{\pi y}{b}\).

Q22. theory, very likely
(a) State the uniqueness theorem and explain its importance. (b) Show that a function satisfying Laplace's equation cannot have a maximum inside a charge-free region. (c) Explain why Laplace's equation, not Poisson's, governs the region between the plates of a charged capacitor.
Show step-by-step solution

(a) "A solution of Poisson's (or Laplace's) equation that satisfies the given boundary conditions is unique." Its importance: any solution, however it was found (guessing, images, separation of variables), is the solution if it satisfies the equation and the boundary conditions. Give the proof sketch from Section 6: \(V_d=V_1-V_2\) gives \(\int|\nabla V_d|^2dv=0\), so \(V_d=0\).

(b) Suppose V had a maximum at a point P. At a maximum, every second derivative is ≤ 0, and for a strict maximum at least one is < 0. Then \(\nabla^2V=V_{xx}+V_{yy}+V_{zz}<0\), which contradicts \(\nabla^2V=0\). Equivalently, by the mean-value property V(P) equals the average over a small sphere around P, so it can't exceed every surrounding value. Therefore the extremes lie on the boundary.

(c) The capacitor's charges are surface charges on the conductors, which are the boundaries. The volume between the plates has \(\rho=0\), so \(\nabla^2V=0\) there. The surface charges enter only through the boundary conditions (V given on each plate) and are recovered afterward via \(\rho_s=\epsilon E_n\).
